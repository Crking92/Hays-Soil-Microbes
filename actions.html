What Should I Do?

Use your soil story to choose actions that protect roots, feed soil life, reduce stress, and match native plants to place.

The simple rule

The best first step is usually not fertilizer. The best first step is matching plants to soil, keeping living roots, protecting soil cover, and avoiding disturbance that breaks soil structure.

Keep roots Roots feed microbes with sugars.
Cover soil Cover reduces heat, drying, crusting, and erosion.
Reduce disturbance Less tilling and compaction protects fungal threads and soil crumbs.
Match plants Native plants work best when soil depth, pH, drainage, and texture fit.

Heavy clay soil

Plain meaning: Sticky soil that can hold water and nutrients, but can compact and become hard when dry.

Do

  • Keep perennial native roots in place.
  • Use mulch or plant cover to reduce crusting.
  • Plant when soil is moist but not wet and sticky.

Avoid

  • Do not work clay when wet.
  • Do not drive equipment repeatedly over wet soil.
  • Do not assume poor growth always means low fertility.

Microbe story

Clay can protect organic matter and microbial habitat inside tiny soil crumbs, but compaction squeezes out air and weakens root-zone life.

Limestone / alkaline soil

Plain meaning: Calcium-rich soil with higher pH. Some nutrients may be present but harder for plants to use.

Do

  • Choose plants adapted to limestone and high pH.
  • Use diverse native roots and organic cover to support nutrient cycling.
  • Use soil tests before adding fertilizer.

Avoid

  • Do not try to permanently force Hill Country limestone soil to become acidic.
  • Do not overuse phosphorus fertilizer without a soil test.

Microbe story

These soils may favor alkaline-tolerant decomposers and nutrient unlockers, especially organisms involved in phosphorus and mineral cycling.

Shallow rocky soil

Plain meaning: Thin soil over rock. Roots have less room and the site dries quickly.

Do

  • Use drought-adapted local native plants.
  • Expect slower establishment and smaller mature size.
  • Protect existing soil from erosion.

Avoid

  • Do not choose water-hungry plants.
  • Do not scrape off thin topsoil.
  • Do not expect deep prairie plants to perform the same as they would in deeper soil.

Microbe story

Wet-dry stress is strong here. Soil life may be shaped by drought survival, root pulses after rain, and rapid drying.

Low organic matter soil

Plain meaning: Soil has less dead plant material and root-fed carbon available for soil organisms.

Do

  • Keep living roots as much of the year as possible.
  • Leave appropriate plant litter where it will not create safety or drainage issues.
  • Use diverse native grasses, forbs, shrubs, and trees where appropriate.

Avoid

  • Do not leave soil bare during hot months.
  • Do not remove every leaf and stem from restoration areas.

Microbe story

Microbes need food. Roots, dead roots, leaves, stems, mulch, and composted organic matter feed the decomposer system.

Wet, lowland, or flood-pulse soil

Plain meaning: Soil may stay wet after rain or experience low oxygen in small pockets.

Do

  • Use plants adapted to periodic saturation if the site floods or drains slowly.
  • Protect streamside vegetation and root mats.
  • Expect different plant choices than upland limestone slopes.

Avoid

  • Do not plant only dry-upland species in wet-bottom areas.
  • Do not compact saturated soil.

Microbe story

Wet soil changes oxygen. That can shift nitrogen cycling, decomposition, and which microbes are most active.

Prairie / grassland signal

Plain meaning: Soil and ecological-site clues suggest a root-driven grassland system.

Do

  • Use diverse native grasses and forbs.
  • Favor deep, fibrous, living root systems.
  • Manage weeds while protecting soil cover.

Avoid

  • Do not treat prairie soil as empty space between plants.
  • Do not remove all standing and fallen plant material unless needed for management.

Microbe story

Grassland roots feed the rhizosphere. Root-partner fungi and bacteria may help with nutrient exchange, soil crumbs, and carbon movement.

Woodland / oak signal

Plain meaning: Soil and plant clues suggest woody roots, leaf litter, shade, or oak-associated processes.

Do

  • Protect leaf litter where it is ecologically appropriate.
  • Avoid soil compaction under trees.
  • Protect root zones during construction or trail work.

Avoid

  • Do not scrape soil clean around mature trees.
  • Do not repeatedly disturb the upper root zone.

Microbe story

Woody plant soils often rely heavily on fungi and litter decomposition. Oak systems may support different root-partner fungi than open prairie soils.

Beginner shortcut: do this first / avoid this for now

When a new gardener feels stuck, reduce the choice overload. Pick the closest soil situation and start with the safest first moves.

Sticky clay

First: cover soil, plant deep-rooted natives, wait until moist-not-sticky to dig.

Avoid: sand, repeated tilling, traffic when wet.

Rocky or shallow

First: choose drought-adapted local natives and protect thin topsoil.

Avoid: water-hungry plants and scraping soil clean.

Limestone / high pH

First: choose limestone-adapted natives and use a soil test before fertilizer.

Avoid: trying to permanently acidify the whole site.

Wet low spot

First: confirm whether it is truly wet or just compacted; pick wet-tolerant plants if repeated.

Avoid: compacting saturated soil.

Bare crusty soil

First: add cover with mulch, leaves, nurse plants, grasses, or groundcovers.

Avoid: leaving soil exposed through summer storms and heat.

Runoff / erosion

First: slow water with roots, surface cover, and contour-aware planting.

Avoid: loose bare soil in concentrated flow paths.

Beginner planting recipes

Clay prairie pocket

Structure: 40% native grasses, 50% flowering perennials, 10% shrubs or accents.

Soil care: plant in fall when possible, keep cover, avoid wet digging.

Rocky limestone bed

Structure: tough bunchgrasses, low forbs, small shrubs, and open rock pockets.

Soil care: protect thin soil and choose high-pH adapted plants.

Shade / oak edge

Structure: leaf-litter-tolerant perennials, sedges, shrubs, and tree-root protection.

Soil care: avoid scraping leaves and compacting the root zone.

Stormwater strip

Structure: dense grasses/sedges first, then deep-rooted forbs and shrubs where space allows.

Soil care: spread water out, slow it down, and armor bare soil fast.

Before adding fertilizer

Use a real soil test for fertilizer decisions. This dashboard can explain soil habitat and possible microbial functions, but it does not measure current nutrient availability at one exact property.

Action guidance is broad soil-health interpretation, not a property-specific prescription.
